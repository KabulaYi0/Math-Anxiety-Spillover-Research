Introduction

Mathematics anxiety is a feeling of tension, apprehension, or fear that interferes with mathematical performance (Ashcraft & Kirk, 2001). It is one of the most thoroughly documented emotional barriers to learning. Large international assessments suggest that a sizeable share of secondary students feel anxious about mathematics, and that this anxiety is reliably linked to weaker mathematics achievement across very different national systems (Foley et al., 2017; OECD, 2013). Researchers no longer view the problem as a simple by-product of low ability. Anxiety about mathematics appears to be a partly independent cause of underperformance, and it pushes students away from the subject and the careers that depend on it (Dowker et al., 2016; Maloney & Beilock, 2012).

The consequences reach past test scores. Students who feel anxious tend to disengage, skip optional quantitative courses, and avoid science, technology, engineering, and mathematics pathways, which narrows their later choices (Maloney & Beilock, 2012; OECD, 2013). Because these avoidance habits form during adolescence, the secondary years are an important time to ask how far the influence of this anxiety actually extends.

Almost all of the existing work, though, keeps the question inside a single subject. It asks how anxiety about mathematics affects mathematics. Students, however, do not experience their timetable as a set of sealed boxes. Secondary schooling now folds quantitative reasoning into many courses, including graphs and statistics in geography and biology, formulae in physics and chemistry, and numerical reasoning in economics and business studies. The cognitive and emotional systems that mathematics anxiety disrupts are not subject specific either.

Does anxiety that begins in mathematics spill over and shape how students feel and perform in their other subjects?

There are good theoretical reasons to expect that it might. On the cognitive side, anxiety about mathematics consumes working memory through intrusive worry, leaving fewer resources for any demanding task (Ashcraft & Kirk, 2001; Eysenck et al., 2007), and nothing about that mechanism confines it to numbers. On the emotional side, the appraisals of control and value that drive achievement emotions, along with the academic self-concepts beneath them, can generalise across related subjects (Pekrun, 2006). There is also a concrete precedent. Work on statistics anxiety shows that students carry mathematics-related apprehension into a neighbouring quantitative field (Paechter et al., 2017).

The topic matters for both theory and practice. Theoretically, showing whether and how this anxiety generalises would sharpen our models of achievement emotion and settle whether mathematics anxiety is a narrow phenomenon or the visible edge of something broader. Practically, the stakes are real. If the anxiety spills over, then programmes that reduce it could pay off well beyond the mathematics room, and treating it as the mathematics department's problem alone would badly understate its cost. Given that this anxiety tends to emerge and harden across the move from primary to secondary school (Cargnelutti et al., 2017), and that it can be passed on by anxious teachers and parents (Beilock et al., 2010), the secondary phase is a fitting and under-studied place to look for spillover.

Literature Review

3.1 What is known about mathematics anxiety

Mathematics anxiety has been studied for more than sixty years, and it is dependably tied to weaker mathematics performance. Two separate meta-analyses put the correlation in the moderate, negative range (Hembree, 1990; Ma, 1999). The relationship also runs in both directions. Poor performance can breed anxiety, anxiety can drag down performance, and over time each feeds the other (Carey et al., 2016). The leading cognitive explanation points to working memory. Anxious thoughts occupy limited executive and storage capacity, so performance suffers most on the problems that demand the most mental effort (Ashcraft & Kirk, 2001; Ramirez et al., 2013, 2018). Brain imaging backs up the emotional weight of the experience: for highly anxious individuals, simply anticipating mathematics activates neural networks associated with pain and threat (Lyons & Beilock, 2012; Suárez-Pellicioni et al., 2016). This account fits closely with Attentional Control Theory, which holds that anxiety pulls attention toward threat and weakens the goal-directed attentional system (Eysenck et al., 2007). That mechanism, by its nature, is not specific to mathematical content, which is precisely why spillover is plausible.

3.2 Measurement and the distinctiveness of mathematics anxiety

A second important development concerns measurement. Researchers have shown that anxiety about mathematics is a distinct construct rather than a relabelling of general nerves. The Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale (Hopko et al., 2003) and its version for children and adolescents, the modified Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale (Carey et al., 2017), have both been validated in large samples. Carey et al. (2017) found that the items load onto a factor separable from test anxiety and from general anxiety. Devine et al. (2012) reported that mathematics anxiety still predicted mathematics performance after test anxiety was controlled, and Hill et al. (2016) showed that it is distinguishable from general anxiety in both primary and secondary students while growing stronger with age. This distinctiveness is the precondition for any claim about subject-specific spillover. Only by measuring and statistically holding constant general and test anxiety can a study credibly attribute effects in other subjects to mathematics anxiety in particular.

3.3 Evidence that hints at spillover

Several lines of work point toward cross-subject transfer without testing it directly. First, mathematics anxiety correlates with general and test anxiety, which suggests a shared emotional base (Carey et al., 2017; Hembree, 1990). Second, the statistics anxiety literature shows students importing mathematics-related apprehension into a related quantitative field. Paechter et al. (2017) found that mathematics anxiety and statistics anxiety share components yet also make independent contributions to statistics performance, so transfer and subject-specificity appear together. Third, theory predicts that spillover could be positive or negative. Pekrun's (2006) control-value theory implies that anxiety should generalise to subjects that share low control and high threat appraisals. Marsh's (1986) internal and external frame of reference model points the other way for verbal subjects, because a weak mathematics self-concept can lift verbal self-concept by contrast, which would dampen or even reverse spillover into language and humanities courses. These competing predictions remain largely untested for mathematics anxiety in secondary school, and they are the reason this study separates math-adjacent from math-distant subjects instead of lumping all other subjects together.

A maths-specific question

The study tests pathways from mathematics anxiety to two kinds of destination. Math-adjacent subjects, such as science and geography, are rich in graphs, formulae, and data. Math-distant subjects, such as languages and humanities, may show weaker effects, or even effects in the opposite direction. Throughout, general and test anxiety are held constant so that any pathway can be read as mathematics anxiety spillover rather than generalised worry.

3.4 Gaps and contribution

Three gaps follow from this review, and this study is built to address all three.

Gap 1

A single-subject focus

Anxiety about mathematics is studied overwhelmingly against mathematics outcomes, so direct spillover across several subjects is rarely examined.

Gap 2

Specificity is rarely isolated

Studies seldom control at once for general anxiety, test anxiety, and prior attainment, so apparent spillover may simply be generalised anxiety in disguise.

Gap 3

Mechanism and meaning sit apart

Quantitative work cannot explain why spillover happens, while the small qualitative literature does not test how far it generalises.

This study takes on all three. It measures the association between mathematics anxiety and outcomes in both math-adjacent and math-distant subjects. It isolates subject-specific effects by controlling for general anxiety, test anxiety, and prior attainment. And it uses a sequential mixed-methods design so that students' own accounts can explain the statistical pattern, including the cases that do not fit. The contribution is a fuller, curriculum-wide picture of how far mathematics anxiety reaches, with direct implications for whole-school approaches to anxiety and for the returns we should expect from interventions.

Research Questions

Overarching question

To what extent, and through what mechanisms, does mathematics anxiety shape secondary students' anxiety, attitudes, and performance in non-mathematics subjects?

  1. Quantitative, extent and specificity. Does mathematics anxiety predict anxiety, attitudes, and achievement in (a) math-adjacent subjects such as science and (b) math-distant subjects such as languages and humanities, after controlling for general anxiety, test anxiety, and prior attainment?
  2. Quantitative, mechanism. Is any spillover that does appear mediated by academic self-concept and by perceived working-memory load, such as worry and distraction during non-mathematics lessons?
  3. Qualitative, meaning. How do secondary students experience and explain mathematics-related anxiety when it surfaces in non-mathematics subjects, and where do they think the boundary of mathematical content lies?

Research Design and Methodology

5.1 Overall design

The study uses a mixed-methods, explanatory sequential design (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018). A quantitative phase establishes whether spillover exists, how large it is, and what mediates it. A following qualitative phase then explains those results through students' accounts. Priority rests with the quantitative strand, and the qualitative strand is woven in to interpret and contextualise the findings. The main point of integration is that interview participants are drawn directly from the quantitative results, as described below.

Phase 1 · Quantitative

Survey and school records

250 to 350 secondary students complete validated anxiety and self-concept scales, including the modified Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale. Grades come from school records. Multilevel and mediation models isolate a maths-specific effect.

Integration

Joint display

5.2 Theoretical and conceptual framework

Two complementary frameworks shape the design. Attentional Control Theory (Eysenck et al., 2007), together with the working-memory account of mathematics anxiety (Ashcraft & Kirk, 2001), motivates the cognitive hypothesis behind the second research question. Because anxiety should impair any task that taxes working memory, this view predicts positive spillover into cognitively demanding subjects. Control-value theory (Pekrun, 2006), alongside Marsh's (1986) internal and external frame of reference model, frames the emotional pathway and generates competing predictions about the direction of spillover across math-adjacent and math-distant subjects. Together the two frameworks decide which variables are measured and how the mediation model is built.

Attentional Control Theory

Anxiety pulls attention toward threat and drains working memory, a mechanism that is not specific to numbers, predicting spillover into other demanding subjects (Eysenck et al., 2007).

Control-Value Theory

Emotions about learning grow from how much control and value students feel, appraisals that can generalise across related subjects (Pekrun, 2006).

Internal/External Frame

A weak mathematics self-concept can raise verbal self-concept by contrast, so spillover into language subjects may be dampened or even reversed (Marsh, 1986).

5.3 Population, setting, and sampling frame

The target population is secondary-school students of roughly thirteen to sixteen years of age. The setting is two or three secondary schools within one local school district, chosen so that students take a broad spread of subjects. The sampling frame is the enrolled students in the selected year groups whose parents or guardians give consent and who themselves assent. For the quantitative phase the target sample is about 250 to 350 students, a figure to be confirmed by an a priori power analysis for detecting small to moderate effects in a regression and mediation model with several covariates, and adjusted for the nesting of students within classes and schools. For the qualitative phase, 16 to 24 students will be purposively sampled from the survey respondents. The selection will deliberately contrast students high in mathematics anxiety who show strong spillover with equally anxious students who show little, alongside a lower-anxiety comparison group. Recruitment begins with gatekeeper approval from each school, followed by class information sessions and the distribution of consent and assent forms. Interview participants are then invited by letter or email on the basis of their survey profiles.

5.4 Data collection

The quantitative phase uses a self-report survey completed in class, built from validated instruments. Mathematics anxiety is measured with the modified Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale (Carey et al., 2017). General or trait anxiety and test anxiety are measured with short established scales so that subject-specific effects can be isolated, following the logic of Carey et al. (2017). Anxiety, attitudes, and self-concept in the comparison subjects are captured with parallel items, including achievement-emotion items adapted from the Achievement Emotions Questionnaire (Pekrun et al., 2011), which will be piloted (see Appendix B). Achievement is taken from the most recent school grades or standardised assessment scores in mathematics and the comparison subjects, drawn from school records to give objective outcomes and reduce common-method bias, together with a prior-attainment indicator.

The qualitative phase uses semi-structured individual interviews of roughly thirty to forty minutes, guided by a schedule aligned to the survey constructs (Appendix C). The interviews explore lived experience, the triggers students notice in non-mathematics lessons, any avoidance behaviour, and where students place the boundary of what counts as mathematics.

5.5 Data analysis

For the quantitative strand, the first step is descriptive and reliability analysis, reporting Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega. To address the first research question, hierarchical multiple regression, or multilevel models that respect the nested structure, will test whether mathematics anxiety predicts each non-mathematics outcome once general anxiety, test anxiety, and prior attainment are entered as covariates. To address the second question, a path or structural equation model will test academic self-concept and perceived working-memory load as mediators of the link between mathematics anxiety and non-mathematics outcomes.

For the qualitative strand, interviews will be transcribed and analysed with reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2021), combining deductive codes drawn from the two frameworks with inductive codes that emerge from the data.

Integration happens in a joint display that sets the statistical results beside the themes that explain them, with close attention to the cases that departed from the model. Where the model flags highly anxious students who show little spillover, for instance, their interviews may reveal coping strategies or a narrow personal definition of mathematics that the survey could not capture. Using qualitative depth to interpret quantitative pattern, anomalies included, is the reason for choosing a sequential rather than a parallel design.

5.6 Ethical considerations

This proposal is designed to meet the standards of the Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans, known as TCPS 2 (Canadian Institutes of Health Research, Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada, & Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada, 2022). No data will be collected before the relevant Research Ethics Board has reviewed and approved the study. The design follows the three core principles of TCPS 2. Respect for persons is addressed through voluntary, informed participation. Because the participants are minors, the study seeks consent from a parent or guardian as the authorised third party and also seeks the assent of each student, who may decline or withdraw at any point without penalty. Concern for welfare guides the handling of a sensitive topic. Questions about anxiety can cause discomfort, so the materials are worded carefully, participation is genuinely optional, and every participant receives a debrief sheet that signposts school and community support services. Justice is reflected in fair, inclusive recruitment across classes and in steps to manage the power imbalance between an adult researcher and young participants, including clear language and the freedom to stop. Privacy and confidentiality are protected by separating identifying information from responses, storing linked records securely, using identifiers in place of names when grades are matched to surveys, and reporting only aggregate or de-identified findings.

5.7 Limitations and anticipated challenges

Several limitations deserve acknowledgement. The quantitative strand is cross-sectional, so it cannot fully settle whether mathematics anxiety causes spillover or whether difficulty in other subjects feeds the anxiety (Carey et al., 2016); results will be framed as conditional associations, and a time-lagged follow-up is noted as future work. Even with controls in place, unmeasured factors such as overall ability or socioeconomic status may shape the results. Self-report measures carry the risk of common-method bias, which the objective grades partly offset, though the emotional measures remain self-reported. Spillover may also differ in sign across subjects, as the internal and external frame of reference model implies, so the analysis is built to detect direction rather than assume it. Practically, the study depends on school access, adequate consent rates, and reliable records, and the qualitative sample is purposive and not statistically generalisable.

Appendix A. Literature search and screening process

The search was documented throughout, following the University of British Columbia Library guidance on keeping track of literature searches (https://guides.library.ubc.ca/litreviews/KeepTrack). I recorded the database, date, search terms, limits, controlled vocabulary, number of results, and comments for every search, and I managed all records in Zotero, which removed duplicates and generated the reference list.

Four databases were searched (ERIC, APA PsycINFO, Scopus, and Web of Science), with Google Scholar used for forward and backward citation tracing on the key papers. The general inclusion criteria were: a peer-reviewed source; published 1990 to 2026, with seminal earlier work retained; a secondary-age or directly relevant sample; and a focus on mathematics anxiety together with either an underlying mechanism or a cross-subject outcome. The exclusion criteria were: not peer reviewed; not in English; off-topic; or duplicate.

Appendix B. Sample survey instrument (excerpt)

Section 1, mathematics anxiety (modified Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale; Carey et al., 2017). For example, "How anxious would you feel listening to the teacher explain a new mathematics topic?" rated from 1 (low anxiety) to 5 (high anxiety). The full licensed scale will be inserted.

Section 2, other-subject anxiety, parallel and piloted. For example, "How anxious would you feel reading a graph in geography?", "interpreting data in a science lesson?", or "analysing a source in history?"

Section 3, academic self-concept by subject. For example, "I am good at science," "I am good at history."

Section 4, worry and distraction during lessons, as a proxy for working-memory load.

Section 5, controls, with brief general-anxiety and test-anxiety items.

Section 6, background, covering year group, gender, and subjects taken, plus a consent reference (grades are obtained from records).

Appendix C. Interview guide (excerpt)
  1. Tell me about how you feel in your mathematics lessons.
  2. Are there other subjects where you get a similar feeling? Can you give an example?
  3. When something that feels like mathematics comes up in science or geography, what goes through your mind?
  4. Are there subjects you try to avoid because they feel too mathematical? Where do you draw that line?
  5. Has worrying in one lesson ever affected how you did in another? Tell me about a time that happened.
Appendix D. Consent and recruitment materials

A parent or guardian information sheet and consent form, a student assent form, a school gatekeeper letter, and a participant debrief sheet with support signposting. These will be appended.
